# Testimonial Request Emails — Joni K. Roberts Law and Mediation Office

Five templates, a consent form, and the website disclaimer language. Read the first section before sending anything — it determines who should get an email at all.

---

## Before you send: two problems, not one

**Anonymity is the smaller problem. Confidentiality is the bigger one.**

Anonymity is about what name appears under the quote. Confidentiality is about whether the firm can publish anything about the representation at all. RPC 1.6 covers information relating to the representation, and in family law that can include the bare fact that someone was a client. A testimonial is the firm publishing that information — so it needs the client's informed consent, in writing, regardless of whether their name is attached.

This matters more than it sounds. Removing the name does not remove the confidentiality issue, and in a market the size of Memphis it often doesn't even deliver real anonymity. "Joni helped me keep my kids when my ex tried to relocate out of state" identifies a person to everyone in their circle, whether it's signed "Clara H." or "Anonymous."

**Who to email**

- Matters that are fully concluded. Never solicit mid-representation — the client is dependent on you at that moment and can't freely decline.
- Clients whose outcome was genuinely good and who have already said something positive unprompted. Those are your yes's.
- Mediation participants only if you were counsel, not if you served as the neutral. A testimonial from one side of a mediation you neutrally conducted undercuts the impartiality the role requires.

**Who not to email**

- Domestic violence clients. Publication can create a real safety risk, and an abuser recognizing a story is not a hypothetical.
- Adoption, paternity, and juvenile matters — sealed or confidential records, and children who didn't consent to anything.
- Anyone whose matter involved allegations they'd want buried.
- Anyone who lost, or partly lost. Obvious, but worth stating.
- Current clients, per above.

**Three rules for every email below**

1. **Offer nothing in exchange.** No gift cards, no discounts, no fee credits. RPC 7.2 restricts giving anything of value to someone for recommending your services. A thank-you note is fine; a $25 Starbucks card is not.
2. **Make declining easy and consequence-free.** Say so explicitly. It protects the client and it protects you.
3. **Approve the final wording with them before it publishes.** Not the draft they send — the version that actually goes live, in the place it goes live. This is the step most firms skip and the one that prevents nearly every problem.

## Template 1 — Standard request (concluded matter, client likely comfortable being named)

**Subject:** A quick favor, if you're willing

Hi [First Name],

I hope you and [your family / the kids] are doing well since we wrapped things up in [month].

I'm updating the firm's website, and I'd like it to reflect what working with me is actually like rather than the usual marketing language. If you'd be willing to write a few sentences about your experience, I'd be grateful.

There's no wrong way to do it. If it helps, here are a few things people often find easier to answer than a blank page:

- What was going on when you first reached out?
- What were you most worried about?
- What was it like working together?
- What would you say to someone in a similar position who's deciding whether to call a lawyer?

A short paragraph is plenty. Two sentences is plenty.

A few things I want to be clear about:

- **This is entirely optional.** If you'd rather not, just say no or ignore this email — it changes nothing between us, and I won't follow up about it again.
- **You control how you're identified.** Full name, first name and last initial, initials only, or nothing at all — your choice.
- **Nothing publishes without your approval.** I'll send you the exact wording, in the exact place it will appear, and it doesn't go live until you say yes.
- **I'll need your written okay.** I've attached a short consent form for that. Boring, but it's how I make sure I'm handling your information properly.

Thank you either way — for trusting me with [your case / this] in the first place.

Warmly,
Joni

## Template 2 — Anonymity-forward (sensitive matter, or client you expect to hesitate)

Same request, different center of gravity. This one leads with anonymity instead of treating it as a footnote, which makes yes easier for people who want to help but don't want their divorce on the internet.

**Subject:** Would you be open to this? (Anonymous is completely fine)

Hi [First Name],

I hope things have settled down for you since [month].

I'm rewriting the firm's website and would like it to include a few words from people I've actually represented. I wanted to ask whether you'd be open to that — **and to say up front that anonymous is completely fine.** Most people prefer it, and it's the default I'd assume unless you tell me otherwise.

If you're willing, a few sentences about what the experience was like would mean a lot. Something as simple as what you were worried about going in, and how it felt to have someone in your corner.

How this would work:

- **You choose the attribution.** "A family law client in Memphis," your initials, your first name only, or no attribution at all. I'll default to the most private option you're comfortable with.
- **I'll take out identifying details.** Anything about the specific outcome, dates, where you live, or the other party comes out before it publishes. If a detail makes the story recognizable to someone who knows you, it doesn't go in — even if you're fine with it.
- **You approve the final version.** I'll send you exactly what would appear, where it would appear, and nothing goes live until you confirm.
- **You can withdraw it later.** If you change your mind in six months or six years, email me and it comes down. No explanation needed.
- **Saying no is completely fine.** Genuinely. If this isn't something you want to do, just say so or let this email sit — it doesn't affect anything, and I won't ask again.

I've attached a short consent form covering all of the above. If you'd rather talk it through first, call me any time.

Thank you for considering it, and thank you for trusting me with something difficult.

Warmly,
Joni

## Template 3 — Google review request

Keep this one separate from the website testimonial ask. A Google review is the client publishing their own words on their own account — different mechanics, and much better for local search visibility than an on-site testimonial.

**Subject:** Would you leave a short review?

Hi [First Name],

I hope you're doing well.

If you have two minutes and felt good about how things went, would you consider leaving a short review on Google? It's one of the main ways people in Memphis find a lawyer, and a few honest sentences from someone who's been through it helps more than anything I could write myself.

Here's the direct link: [INSERT GOOGLE REVIEW LINK]

Two notes:

- **Write only what you're comfortable making public.** A Google review is fully public and under your own account name, and I can't edit or remove it. Please don't include details about your case you'd want kept private.
- **No obligation at all.** If you'd rather not, that's completely fine and I won't bring it up again.

Thank you — for this, and for trusting me with your case.

Warmly,
Joni

> **Important, for you rather than the client:** if a negative review ever appears, do not respond with any facts about the representation. Lawyers have been disciplined in multiple states for defending themselves online by disclosing case details. A public review by a client does not waive RPC 1.6. The safe response is a short, factless line — something like "I take all feedback seriously and would welcome the chance to speak directly. Please call the office." — or no response at all.

Send once, roughly two weeks later. If there's no reply to this, the answer is no. Don't send a third.

**Subject:** Re: A quick favor, if you're willing

Hi [First Name],

Just floating this back up in case it got buried — no pressure at all, and this is the last you'll hear from me about it.

If you'd still like to write something, I'd be glad to have it. And if not, that's genuinely fine.

## A few practical notes

**Expect a low response rate, and don't fight it.** Twenty emails might produce five testimonials. That's normal, and five real ones beat twenty manufactured ones by a wide margin.

**Four to six is plenty.** More than that and visitors stop reading. Spread them across practice areas rather than stacking them all on divorce.

**Specific beats glowing.** "I called on a Thursday and had an appointment Monday" does more work than "Joni is the best attorney in Memphis." AI assistants and human readers both discount superlatives and retain concrete detail — same principle as the website copy.

**Watch the results language.** The old testimonials on the site leaned on outcomes ("saved my business," "won my custody battle"). Those are the ones most likely to read as creating unjustified expectations under RPC 7.1. Steer clients toward process and experience — responsiveness, clarity, feeling heard — rather than what they got.

**Keep the records.** Signed consent forms, the approval emails, and the original submissions. RPC 7.2 requires retaining advertising materials for two years; keeping the consent alongside them is just sensible.

**One caveat:** I've flagged the confidentiality and advertising issues that bear on this, but I'm not your ethics counsel. Testimonials from family law clients sit in a genuinely sensitive area of RPC 1.6, and it's worth a call to the Board's ethics counsel at 1-800-486-5714 before the first one goes live. It's free, it's confidential, and it takes ten minutes.
